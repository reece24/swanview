Counterpulsation

A balloon in the descending aorta fills with helium in diastole and empties just before systole, opposite to the heart's rhythm.

Inflates at the dicrotic notchPushes blood back toward the aortic root and forward to the body. Diastolic pressure rises, and so does coronary perfusion, which happens in diastole.
Deflates just before systoleLeaves an empty space in the aorta. End-diastolic pressure drops, so the LV ejects against less afterload and uses less oxygen.

Typical effect: cardiac output up about 0.5–1 L/min and wedge down about 20%. In IABP-SHOCK II (600 patients with MI and cardiogenic shock), routine use did not lower 30-day mortality.

Reading the 1:2 tracing

Check timing at 1:2 so each assisted beat sits next to an unassisted one.

  1. Unassisted systole. The patient's own beat.
  2. Dicrotic notch. Aortic valve closes; inflation starts here, making a sharp V.
  3. Augmented diastole. Should be higher than unassisted systole.
  4. Assisted end-diastole. The dip at deflation, about 10–15 lower than unassisted end-diastole.
  5. Assisted systole. About 5 lower than unassisted systole, because afterload fell.

Who gets one

Used for

Cardiogenic shock with ischemia, acute MR or VSR as a bridge to surgery, refractory ischemia, and failure to wean from bypass.

Avoid with

More than mild aortic regurgitation (diastolic inflation worsens it), aortic dissection or aneurysm, severe peripheral arterial disease, and uncontrolled bleeding.

Trigger

Usually the ECG R wave. The arterial pressure can trigger instead (a poor ECG, or CPR). Pacer mode needs a fully paced rhythm. Tracking suffers above about 150 bpm; consider 1:2.

Where the tip belongs

The radiopaque tip should sit in the proximal descending aorta, just below the left subclavian artery. The bottom of the balloon should end above the renal arteries.

The aortic knob is less reliable: in one CT study, a tip placed at the knob was too close to the left subclavian in 16% of patients, while 2 cm above the carina was adequate in 95%.

Check it

  • A chest film after insertion, every day, and after the patient is moved or the waveform changes.
  • Left radial pulse (or a left radial arterial line) for a balloon that has moved up.
  • Urine output, creatinine, lactate and abdominal exam for a balloon that has moved down.
  • Pulses and perfusion in the leg with the sheath.

Weaning

Weaning means giving fewer assisted beats (or less volume) and watching whether the patient holds up.

Ready when

Minimal inotropes, cardiac index above 2, SvO₂ above 65%, no active ischemia, and adequate urine output.

How

Step from 1:1 to 1:2, then 1:3 (or 1:4). Hold each step for at least 30 minutes and reassess before the next. Some units reduce balloon volume instead.

Watch

MAP, heart rate, cardiac index, SvO₂, wedge, lactate, urine output, chest pain and new ST changes.

Stop the wean if

Hypotension, rising wedge, falling SvO₂ or output, rising lactate, or ischemia. Go back to the last ratio that worked.

Don't

Leave it in standby or at a low ratio for long, especially without anticoagulation. A still balloon collects clot.

Show each ratio on the console

Every day

  • Limb: pulses, color, temperature and the sheath site. Limb ischemia is the most common major complication.
  • Labs: platelets (a mild fall is common and recovers after removal), hemoglobin, creatinine, lactate.
  • Film: tip position.
  • Numbers: take MAP from the console. Bedside monitors label the augmented peak as “systolic.”
  • Plan: is the reason for the pump still there? Risk rises with every day of support.
